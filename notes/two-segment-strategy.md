# Two Business Theses — North Star Statement
**Started:** June 2026
**Context:** Emerged from customer discovery work (Tu Rinsche blog analysis) and OIE/NSF I-Corps preparation

---

## The Core Insight

Two distinct business theses, two distinct customer segments, two distinct products. The tension between "staying true to Lucid's vision" and "finding a near-term business need" is resolved by recognizing these are different problems for different customers — and that they are complementary rather than competing.

The I-Corps customer discovery process should test each thesis separately, with separate interview tracks, separate hypotheses, and separate willingness-to-pay assessments.

---

## The Structural Premise

Both theses rest on a shared diagnosis that Tu Rinsche circles without stating plainly: **corporations are the wrong primary vehicle for protecting human rights.** They are powerful, they are the proximate cause of supply chain labor conditions, and they respond to regulatory pressure — but their incentive structures are aligned with shareholders, not workers. This is not a design failure (Tu's "design failure not commitment failure" framing is more charitable than the evidence warrants). It is a structural feature of the corporate form: directors have fiduciary duties to shareholders, and HRDD investments that don't clearly reduce financial risk are structurally difficult to justify.

The implication: private corporate HRDD programs have a ceiling. They will improve when regulatory, reputational, and legal pressure makes non-compliance costly — and not before. When they do improve, it is primarily to manage corporate exposure, not to advance worker welfare as a primary goal.

**This is the premise of both theses, not a critique of them:**
- Thesis 1 (Lucid Ledger) is designed to work *without* corporate cooperation. The public ledger produces verifiable evidence for accountability actors regardless of what the corporation discloses. Its customers are the enforcement actors who step in precisely when private corporate governance fails.
- Thesis 2 (HRDD Consultancy) serves corporations honestly — not by helping them "do HRDD better for workers," but by helping them demonstrate defensible compliance to external scrutiny while retaining maximum operational control. That is what corporate buyers actually want, and there is nothing wrong with selling it clearly.

The two theses are not in tension. They operate on different sides of the same accountability relationship.

## Business Thesis 1 — Lucid Ledger (PBC, with partners)

**The thesis:**
*"Global supply chains lack verifiable, tamper-evident records of employment relationships and wage payments. A public blockchain platform that records hiring, contracts, and payments — accessible to workers, brands, regulators, and civil society — would enable genuine accountability that audit-based compliance cannot provide. The market trigger is regulatory enforcement (CSDDD, UFLPA) and anchor buyers in high-scrutiny sectors."*

**Customer:** Explicitly *not* corporations as the primary customer. The customers are the **accountability ecosystem**: regulators (CBP, EU enforcement bodies), development finance institutions (IFC, FMO, Proparco) conditioning lending on verified labor standards, labor-sending governments (Nepal DOFE, Philippines DOLE, Indonesia MOMT) protecting their workers abroad, civil society organizations building litigation and advocacy cases, and brands under hard legal mandate who need a compliance evidence trail they cannot fabricate. These actors share one need: evidence they do not have to take a corporation's word for.

A secondary corporate customer class exists, but it is defined by situation, not by sector: **corporations in "hair on fire" regulatory or legal exposure.** A brand facing an active UFLPA WRO, a CBP detention, an EU FLR investigation, or credible litigation (see the Dyson case in Section VIII of SYNTHESIS) needs verified, tamper-evident compliance evidence it cannot produce through self-reporting or standard audits. In high-scrutiny sectors — fishing, mining, agriculture, Gulf construction — where forced labor is severe and enforcement is tightening, the cost of non-compliance can exceed the cost of adopting genuine verification infrastructure. In these situations a corporate buyer will approach Lucid as risk management, not as virtue. That is a legitimate customer relationship and should remain an open pitch option. The structural premise holds — the motivation is avoiding liability, not advancing worker welfare — but the outcome (adoption of verifiable infrastructure) is the same regardless of motivation. These are not the modal corporate buyer; they are the exception where corporate interest and accountability actor interest happen to align.

A tertiary customer class: **labor-sending governments with market access incentives.** Thailand, Vietnam, Malaysia, and Bangladesh all depend on EU and US market access for their export sectors. If those governments can demonstrate to EU or US regulators that their export industries operate on verified, tamper-evident labor compliance infrastructure, they have a credible defense against import bans and WROs. Lucid is the tool that makes that argument verifiable rather than self-reported. The government becomes the anchor customer that mandates adoption in its export sector.

**Why Lucid fits:** The PBC model, open source ethos, public ledger, and escrow mechanism are exactly what this segment needs. This is not a compromise architecture — it is the right architecture for stakeholders who cannot afford to take a corporation's word for it. Corporations may use Lucid data and benefit from it, but designing the platform to serve corporate interests first would undermine the independence that gives Lucid its value to accountability actors.

**Governance:** PBC, open source, with research advisors. The Posit/RStudio model — public infrastructure + academic research outputs + eventual consultancy revenue.

**Funding path:** Impact investors, IFC, USAID, EU Horizon, foundations. Not B2B SaaS revenue. Pilots in high-scrutiny sectors (fishing, palm oil, construction) with an anchor buyer or development finance co-investment.

**Timeline:** Medium-to-long. Regulatory triggers (EU CSDDD, forced labor tariffs, CBP WROs) are building toward mandatory verifiable claims. No near-term revenue expected. This is the right home for Manny's academic interests and coding/data science skills.

**Academic fit:** The platform generates novel data on employment relationships, wage payment patterns, and labor market dynamics that feeds directly into publishable research.

## Business Thesis 2 — HRDD Measurement & Dashboard Consultancy (solo, private)

**The thesis:**
*"HRDD practitioners inside companies have compliance obligations but no rigorous framework for measuring whether their programs are working. They cannot answer the question 'is this working?' because nobody has defined what success looks like or how to track it. A research-grounded KPI framework, deployed first as a consulting service and eventually as a dashboard product, fills that gap."*

**The specific gap:** Tu Rinsche and practitioners like her keep naming outcomes they want to track — grievance resolution rates, wage theft complaints, zero tolerance incidents — but have no methodology for constructing those metrics, identifying the underlying data, or tracking progress over time. CHRB covers policy commitments, not outcomes. The OECD six-step framework says what to DO, not how to know if it's working. This gap is real in both practitioner and academic literature.

**Customer:** Corporate HRDD practitioners, sustainability leads, compliance teams at $100M+ brands. The person Tu Rinsche writes for. They are not ideologically opposed to blockchain but cannot sell it internally — their job is to get something working now, make the case at budget time, and produce reports that satisfy regulators and investors.

**The honest value proposition:** *"Regulatory enforcement (CSDDD, EU FLR, UFLPA) and civil society scrutiny are raising the evidentiary standard for HRDD. I help companies design measurement systems that produce defensible compliance evidence — so that when regulators, investors, or civil society ask 'is this working?', you have a credible answer that holds up under scrutiny."*

This is subtly different from "I help you do HRDD better for workers." The corporate buyer wants to satisfy external scrutiny while retaining maximum operational control. The measurement framework delivers that. If it also produces better outcomes for workers — which rigorous measurement tends to do, by making problems visible — that is a genuine co-benefit, not the primary sales argument. Selling Thesis 2 as worker welfare advocacy to a corporate buyer is both inaccurate and a harder sell than selling it as regulatory risk management.

**Why this fits Manny's skills:** Defining KPIs rigorously, identifying what data is needed to construct them, handling the denominator problem, dealing with selection bias in grievance data — these are research design and data science problems, not auditing problems. This is NOT a compliance auditing service.

**Governance:** Solo venture, private company, separate from Lucid Ledger governance and advisors.

**Go-to-market:**
1. *Phase 1 — Consulting:* Work with HRDD practitioners to design their KPI frameworks and measurement systems. Manual, high-touch. Builds methodology, client relationships, and the legal/sector knowledge base.
2. *Phase 2 — Tool:* Dashboard product organized around the validated measurement framework. Practitioners helped design it, so they recognize it immediately.
3. *Phase 3 — SaaS:* Automated document ingestion, KPI tracking, CSDDD reporting. Sold as a subscription.

**Funding path:** Consulting fees from Phase 1. Self-funded product development. Eventually B2B SaaS revenue.

**Timeline:** Near-term. The pain is acute today.

**Academic fit:** The KPI framework is publishable — a rigorous measurement methodology for HRDD programs fills a genuine gap in the literature and generates case study data.

## The Virtuous Cycle

These two theses are synergistic rather than competing, and the dependency runs in both directions:

1. **Thesis 2 (dashboard) builds data relationships with brands and suppliers**
2. **Those relationships create compliance pressure that drives suppliers toward Lucid**
3. **Lucid adoption gives Thesis 1 customers (regulators, civil society) the public evidence they've been demanding**
4. **Regulatory/civil society pressure — amplified by the higher evidentiary standard Lucid makes possible — keeps brands investing in Thesis 2 tools**

The commercial product creates demand for the public infrastructure. Lucid becomes the premium verified data source that the dashboard plugs into when suppliers are on-chain.

**The market dependency runs the other way too:** As Lucid raises the evidentiary standard that regulators and civil society expect, Thesis 2 becomes more valuable — because corporations need help designing programs that meet a higher bar. Each thesis creates the market conditions the other needs. Lucid is not just Thesis 2's data source; it is Thesis 2's demand generator. And Thesis 2 is not just a revenue stream; it is Lucid's distribution channel into the corporate segment.

## What This Resolves

- **The skills fit:** Lucid uses Manny's full stack (coding, blockchain, data science, research). The consultancy uses his research design and analytical skills without requiring him to be an auditor.
- **The revenue problem:** Thesis 2 generates near-term income. Thesis 1 is funded through impact investment and pilots while the regulatory environment matures.
- **The "bending Lucid" problem:** No need to strip blockchain and escrow out of Lucid to serve corporate teams. Each product serves its natural constituency.
- **The advisor question:** Lucid remains the shared project with research advisors. The consultancy is a solo venture.
- **The information vs. incentive problem:** Brands may already know about wage suppression but lack the incentive to act. Thesis 2 helps the ones who want to act do so rigorously. Thesis 1 changes the incentive structure by making evidence public and accessible to enforcement actors.

## I-Corps Interview Tracks

**For Thesis 1 (NGOs, government, worker orgs, development finance):**
- What evidence of compliance do you currently have access to, and what would you need to trust it?
- What would change your relationship with corporate HRDD programs — what would make you believe them?
- If a public, verifiable wage payment ledger existed for your sector, how would you use it?
- What would it take for your organization to co-fund or mandate this infrastructure?

**For Thesis 2 (HRDD practitioners, sustainability leads):**
- **What are your KPIs?**
- Walk me through what you did last week. Was that a typical week?
- What does success look like for your HRDD program — and how do you measure it?
- When your CFO asks "is this working?", what do you say?
- What would you pay for a rigorous KPI framework and the tools to track it? Who approves that budget?
